Important Warnings & Expectations

  • You are training an AI on your computer - Everything runs locally, nothing is sent to the cloud.
  • System lag during training - Training an AI is an intensive process, it will push your GPU/CPU and RAM near capacity. Your computer will likely feel very sluggish and hard to use for other tasks until finished. While training is running, Bellows will prevent your computer from automatically going to sleep and pauses normal Fire vs Fire page scanning. You can close this tab and reopen live progress anytime from Fire vs Fire Options. Training will not start unless your device is plugged in.
  • Estimated time on your hardware: Detecting your hardware… (typically between several minutes and a few hours). For heavy models or CPU-only laptops, consider letting it run for a few hours and doing something else, or use a different device.
  • Temporary disk space required: Needs ~1.2 GB free temporarily for Image training or ~0.9 GB for Text to store the base web dataset, ethical guard, and training caches.
  • Results are not guaranteed: Depending on your topic and examples, the classifier might make mistakes, have false alarms, or even fail completely to learn the pattern.
  • Runtime impact per classifier: Every active classifier you add increases the work each time you open or scroll a page, meaning classification will be slower and use more power.

    How Your AI Is Being Made

    Traditionally, computer programs were made by human developers writing instructions, telling the computer step by step how to perform a task. Computers would follow these instructions to the letter, and most of the time it would work—and when it did not, it would glitch and fail to perform the task. However, some tasks are impossible to write down in code: explaining exactly how to handle every scenario a program would need to adapt to in order to speak English or draw a picture of a cute cat simply cannot be written as fixed rules.

    Programmers and scientists have always wanted to make computers perform any task they envision. They realized that whilst they did not know how to explicitly program a computer to speak English, they already knew of a computer that does: the human brain. They reasoned that they knew enough about biological brains to try building a conventional computer program that models something close enough to a brain, and then teach that simulated brain how to perform tasks that are impossible to program directly.

    The human brain is a collection of very small, simple cells called neurons. These cells communicate with each other through many connections that carry electrical signals between them. We still do not understand every detail of how neurons communicate—they do not talk like a conventional computer, as the messages between neurons are capable of representing far more than just a one or a zero while still relying on electricity as the base communication method. Furthermore, we do not fully understand how an individual neuron takes in information and what criteria it uses to process it in order to collectively create consciousness and high-level thought.

    When programmers design an AI, they do not build an exact simulation of a human brain—just as engineers design aeroplanes that are clearly inspired by birds, yet are bigger, use jet engines, and have wings that don't flap. Instead, programmers model the connections between neurons as numbers, and the processing within each neuron as a mathematical function performed on those numbers—for example, multiplying an incoming signal by a weight between -1 and 1. This model, called Artificial Intelligence (AI), is close enough to how biological brains work that it is capable of complex pattern recognition and reasoning. However, a modeled AI brain is far simpler than a biological one, and using current technology it requires far more energy to think and far more effort to train than a human brain.

    When a programmer builds an AI to complete a specific task—as you are doing right now with your classifier—they start with a simulated brain whose neurons are initialized with starting numbers. On its own for your new task, this brain is like a baby outputting random signals that mean nothing. When we show it an example of content from the internet, the simulated brain processes the signals and produces a guess. In order to make the AI do what we want, we show it many examples of the content we want to classify, compare its guesses against the answers we want, and gently tweak the numbers inside the neurons so the AI becomes more likely to output the right classifications next time. The exact internal thought process of how the AI decides what is or isn't your target remains intangible to a human—however, that does not matter, because we can keep nudging those numbers until we arrive at a set of model weights that reliably differentiates between the content you want to filter out and everything else.

    In this interface, you can see how accurate your AI is becoming as we show it all the examples of content that you have gathered. Over time, we will tweak the neurons in its brain to find a set of numbers that work for your desired classifier. The specific shape of the brain of the AI you are training right now is called EfficientNet-B0, and it has 4,008,829 neurons.
